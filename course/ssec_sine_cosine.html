    Worksheet 4.1.3 The Sine and Cosine Functions
    

    Probably the two most important functions in trigonometry are defined based on angles and the coordinates of points on a circle of radius \(1\text{,}\) which we refer to as the unit circle.
    🔗
    For any point, \(P\text{,}\) on the unit circle, we measure the angle for \(P\) in radians from the positive \(x\)-axis counter-clockwise to \(P\text{.}\) Negative angles and angles greater than \(2\pi\) are allowed: negative angles are measured counter-clockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, and angles greater than \(2\pi\) (or less than \(-2\pi\)) start around the circle again.
    🔗
    Figure 12 shows the unit circle with a point \(P=(a,b)\) and its angle, \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now that we have the unit circle and have explained how we measure angles on the unit circle, we are ready to define the circular functions sine and cosine.
    🔗

    Definition 13. The Sine and Cosine Functions.

    For any real number \(\theta\text{,}\) define the point \(P=(a,b)\) on the unit circle whose angle (measured from the positive \(x\)-axis) is \(\theta\) rad. Then the cosine of \(\theta\) is the \(x\)-coordinate of \(P\) and the sine of \(\theta\) is the \(y\)-coordinate of \(P\text{.}\) That is,
    \begin{align*} \cos(\theta)&=a, &\sin(\theta)&=b. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 14.

    We will find \(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\) and \(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We know that \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) is \(45^{\circ}\text{,}\) so we start by drawing a point on the unit circle that makes an angle of \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) with the positive \(x\)-axis and drop a perpendicular from the point to the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    Because we now have a \(45,45,90\) triangle, we know the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates of \(P\) will be the same size, and because we are in the first quadrant, they will both be positive. So, let \(P=(a,a)\text{,}\) where \(a>0\text{,}\) and we will find the value of \(a\text{,}\) and therefore the sine and cosine of \(\frac{\pi}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    The Unit Circle with an Angle, a Point, and a Segment to the x-axis Shown
    🔗
    From the Pythagorean theorem, since the unit circle has radius \(1\) and the radius of the unit circle is the hypotenuse of the right triangle, we have
    \begin{equation*} a^{2}+a^{2}=1^{2} \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} 2a^{2}=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Dividing by \(2\) gives us
    \begin{equation*} a^{2}=\frac{1}{2}, \end{equation*}
    and then taking the (positive) square root give us
    \begin{align*} a&=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\\ &=\frac{\sqrt{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}. \end{align*}
    If we want to rationalize the denominator, we can multiply both the numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{2}\) to get
    \begin{align*} a&=\frac{1\cdot\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}}\\ &=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Therefore, we now know that \(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\) and \(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    1.

    In the following explorations, use symmetry, the Pythagorean theorem, and geometry to relate angles and the values of sine and cosine.
    🔗
    One geometry fact you may not know is the following: in a triangle with angles \(\frac{\pi}{6}=30^{\circ}\text{,}\) \(\frac{\pi}{3}=60^{\circ}\text{,}\) and \(\frac{\pi}{2}=90^{\circ}\) (a 30-60-90 triangle) , the shortest side (opposite the \(30^{\circ}=\frac{\pi}{6}\) angle) is half the length of the hypotenuse (longest side).
    🔗

    (a)

    Find \(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)\) and \(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Dropping a perpendicular from the point at \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) on the unit circle creates a 30-60-90 triangle with the perpendicular being the shortest side. Since the hypotenuse has length \(1\text{,}\) that means that the \(y\)-coordinate of the point is \(\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) and so \(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\) Then, from the Pythagorean theorem, if we let the \(x\)-coordinate of the point be \(a\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} a^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}=1^{2} \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} a^{2}+\frac{1}{4}=1 \end{equation*}
    and therefore \(a^{2}=\frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) Since we know that \(a>0\text{,}\) we know that \(a=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (c)

    Find \(\sin\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\) and \(\cos\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \begin{equation*} \sin\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \cos\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Dropping a perpendicular from the point at \(\frac{5\pi}{6}\) on the unit circle creates a 30-60-90 triangle with the perpendicular being the shortest side. Since the hypotenuse has length \(1\text{,}\) that means that the \(y\)-coordinate of the point is \(\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) and so \(\sin\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\) Then, from the Pythagorean theorem, if we let the \(x\)-coordinate of the point be \(a\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} a^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}=1^{2} \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} a^{2}+\frac{1}{4}=1 \end{equation*}
    and therefore \(a^{2}=\frac{3}{4}\text{.}\) Since the point is in the second quadrant, we know that \(a\lt 0\text{,}\) so we know that \(a=\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\) Thus, \(\cos\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\)
